## Topic 1: Whole Numbers

### 1.1 Place Value Up to Hundred-Thousands

**Step 1 — Build place value from what's already familiar (tens, hundreds) outward**

Every digit's value depends on WHERE it sits. In 45,326: the "4" sits in the ten-thousands spot, so it's worth 40,000 — not just "4." Let's build this step by step using a place value chart:

| Ten-Thousands | Thousands | Hundreds | Tens | Units |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 4 | 5 | 3 | 2 | 6 |

**Worked Example:**
Write 45,326 in expanded form (showing what each digit is really worth):
40,000 + 5,000 + 300 + 20 + 6 = 45,326

**Worked Example (reading a number aloud correctly):**
72,408 — read as "seventy-two thousand, four hundred and eight." Notice: there's a ZERO in the tens place, so we don't say "seventy-two thousand, four hundred and zero eight" — the zero is silent, just a placeholder.

**Practice Questions:**
1. Write 63,915 in expanded form.
2. What is the value of the digit 7 in 27,481?
3. Write in words: 90,204 (careful with the zero — how should it be read?).

### 1.2 Comparing and Ordering Numbers

**Step 1 — Establish the method: compare digit by digit, starting from the LEFT (the biggest place value)**

**Worked Example:**
Compare 34,521 and 34,498. Start from the left: both have "3" in ten-thousands, both have "4" in thousands — tie so far. Next: 5 vs 4 in hundreds — 5 is bigger, so 34,521 > 34,498. We didn't even need to look further right once we found a difference.

**Practice Questions:**
1. Which is bigger: 56,204 or 56,240? Explain using the digit-by-digit method.
2. Order from smallest to largest: 12,890; 12,098; 12,908.

## Topic 2: Addition and Subtraction of Large Whole Numbers

### 2.1 Addition with Regrouping (Carrying)

**Step 1 — Recall WHY we regroup: when a column adds to 10 or more, we can't write two digits in one column, so we carry the extra ten to the next column**

**Worked Example (full column-by-column walkthrough):**
   4,586
 + 2,749
Units: 6+9=15 → write 5, carry 1 (that 1 means "one ten")
Tens: 8+4+1(carried)=13 → write 3, carry 1
Hundreds: 5+7+1=13 → write 3, carry 1
Thousands: 4+2+1=7
Result: 7,335

**Practice Questions:**
1. Add: 3,847 + 2,956, showing every carry.
2. Add: 15,608 + 9,275.

### 2.2 Subtraction with Regrouping (Borrowing)

**Step 1 — Recall WHY we borrow: when the top digit is smaller than the bottom digit in a column, we bring one "ten" over from the next column left**

**Worked Example:**
   6,203
 − 2,847
Units: 3−7, can't do it — borrow from tens: tens digit (0) is also empty, so borrow travels further left to hundreds (2 becomes 1), tens becomes 10, then units borrows from tens (10 becomes 9): 13−7=6
Tens: 9−4=5
Hundreds: 1−8, can't do it — borrow from thousands: thousands (6 becomes 5), hundreds becomes 11: 11−8=3
Thousands: 5−2=3
Result: 3,356

**Practice Questions:**
1. Subtract: 5,402 − 1,867, showing every borrowing step.
2. Subtract: 8,000 − 3,456 (careful — multiple zeros require borrowing to travel far left).

## Topic 3: Multiplication of Whole Numbers

### 3.1 Multiplying by a 2-Digit Number

**Step 1 — Establish that multiplying by a 2-digit number means multiplying by the TENS part and the UNITS part separately, then adding the results**

**Worked Example:**
234 × 23
Break 23 into 20 + 3.
234 × 3 = 702
234 × 20 = 4,680 (this is 234×2, then a zero added since we're really multiplying by 20)
Add: 702 + 4,680 = 5,382

**Practice Questions:**
1. Multiply: 312 × 14, showing the breakdown into tens and units.
2. Multiply: 456 × 32.

## Topic 4: Division of Whole Numbers

### 4.1 Division with Remainders

**Step 1 — Establish what a remainder means: what's LEFT OVER when a number doesn't divide exactly**

**Worked Example:**
47 ÷ 5. How many times does 5 fit into 47? 5×9=45 (fits 9 times), with 47−45=2 left over.
47 ÷ 5 = 9 remainder 2.

**Worked Example (a real-world framing):**
If 47 sweets are shared equally among 5 children, each gets 9 sweets, and 2 sweets are left over (not enough to give each child one more).

**Practice Questions:**
1. Divide: 58 ÷ 6, stating the remainder.
2. 83 oranges are shared among 7 baskets equally. How many oranges per basket, and how many are left over?

## Topic 5: Factors and Multiples (Introductory)

**Step 1 — Establish "factor" using the sharing idea, and "multiple" using repeated counting**

A factor of a number divides into it exactly (no remainder). Multiples of a number are what you get counting up in that number's steps (like counting in 5s: 5,10,15,20...).

**Worked Example:**
Factors of 12: test each number 1-12 for exact division: 1✓,2✓,3✓,4✓,6✓,12✓ (5,7,8,9,10,11 don't divide exactly). Factors: 1,2,3,4,6,12.

**Practice Questions:**
1. List the factors of 18.
2. List the first 5 multiples of 7.
3. Find the LCM of 4 and 6 (recall: list multiples of each, find the first one they share).

## Topic 6: Fractions

### 6.1 Proper, Improper, and Mixed Numbers

**Step 1 — Establish the THREE forms using a visual: a proper fraction is less than one whole; an improper fraction is one whole or more, written as a top-heavy fraction; a mixed number is the same amount as an improper fraction, written as a whole number plus a proper fraction**

**Worked Example (converting improper to mixed):**
7/3 means 7 divided into groups of 3. 7÷3=2 remainder 1, so 7/3 = 2 whole groups plus 1/3 left over = 2⅓.

**Worked Example (converting mixed to improper, the reverse):**
3¼: multiply the whole number by the denominator, then add the numerator: (3×4)+1=13, keep the same denominator: 13/4.

**Practice Questions:**
1. Convert 9/4 to a mixed number.
2. Convert 5⅔ to an improper fraction.
3. Is 8/8 proper, improper, or does it equal exactly one whole? Explain.

### 6.2 Equivalent Fractions

**Worked Example:**
Is 2/3 the same as 6/9? Multiply top and bottom of 2/3 by 3: 2×3=6, 3×3=9 → yes, 2/3=6/9.

**Practice Questions:**
1. Find an equivalent fraction to 3/5 with a denominator of 20.
2. Are 4/6 and 2/3 equivalent? Show your check.
